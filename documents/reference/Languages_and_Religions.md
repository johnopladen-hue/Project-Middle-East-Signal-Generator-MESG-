# Languages and Religions of the Peoples of the Middle East

This document provides a granular overview of the major languages and their dialects spoken by the peoples of the Middle East, refined by the religions and sects they practice. It focuses on traditional heartlands and associated communities.

**Structure**: Language as the main category, dialect/variety as the subcategory. One entry per language/dialect. Religions and sects serve as a refining dimension. Primary living areas refer to traditional concentrations in the Middle East (significant diasporas exist worldwide).

News sources listed are publicly available web outlets that regularly cover the relevant communities, regions, or publish content related to the language/dialect (examples current as of 2026).

## Additional Notes

- **Arabic dialects** form a continuum. The major groupings above (Egyptian, Levantine, Mesopotamian, Gulf/Peninsular, Yemeni) are the standard regional classifications used by linguists. Sub-varieties (e.g., Baghdadi vs. Mosul within Mesopotamian, or Najdi vs. Hejazi within Peninsular) exist but are usually mutually intelligible within groups.
- **Kurdish** is commonly divided into Northern (Kurmanji), Central (Sorani), and Southern groups. Kurmanji and Sorani are the two with the most speakers, media, and standardization.
- Religions often cut across language lines (e.g., Arabic-speaking Christians and Druze; Kurdish-speaking Yazidis). Sectarian identity can be as salient as, or more salient than, dialect.
- News sources are selected for accessibility and relevance. Many (Al Jazeera, Rudaw, SyriacPress, Haaretz, etc.) publish in multiple languages or have English versions. Coverage quality and independence vary by outlet and country.
- Smaller varieties (e.g., Modern South Arabian languages in Yemen/Oman, Zazaki, Balochi, Gilaki/Mazanderani, Circassian) exist but were omitted for focus on higher-speaker or historically prominent ones.

## Broader Context

The Middle East (core: Egypt, the Levant, Arabian Peninsula, Iraq, Iran, Turkey, and sometimes Cyprus) is a historic crossroads. Arabic is the most widely spoken language, followed by Persian, Turkish, Kurdish, and Hebrew. Islam (Sunni majority overall, with large Shia populations and smaller branches such as Alawite, Zaidi, Ibadi, Alevi, and Druze) predominates, alongside ancient Christian communities, Judaism, Yazidism, Mandaeism, Zoroastrianism, and others.

Language and religion do not always align perfectly with ethnicity. Urbanization, migration, education, and politics continue to shape these patterns.
